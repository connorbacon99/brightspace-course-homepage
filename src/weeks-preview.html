Welcome

Every cliff, riverbank and road cut is a record of the past. Geologists read those records the way historians read documents: layer by layer, looking for clues about what happened and when. By the end of this course you'll be able to do the same.

A planet in layers

Earth is not the same all the way through. When the young planet was hot enough to be partly molten, heavy iron sank toward the center and lighter rock rose toward the surface. Geologists call this sorting differentiation, and it left Earth in three main layers.

The crust is the thin outer skin. Continental crust is made mostly of granite-like rock and averages about 35 km thick, reaching 70 km under big mountain ranges. Oceanic crust is denser basalt, only 5 to 10 km thick.

The mantle is about 2,900 km thick and makes up roughly 84% of Earth's volume. It is solid rock, but over millions of years it flows very slowly, like extremely stiff putty. That slow flow drives the plate motions we'll study in Unit 2.

The core is mostly iron and nickel. The outer core is liquid, and its swirling motion generates Earth's magnetic field. The inner core is solid even though it is about 5,200 °C, close to the temperature of the Sun's surface. The pressure there is so great that the metal can't melt.

Seeing through solid rock

Nobody has ever been to the mantle. The deepest hole ever drilled reaches about 12 km, which is 0.2% of the way to the center. Almost everything we know about the deep interior comes from earthquakes.

Earthquakes send out two main kinds of body waves. P waves are compression waves that can travel through solids and liquids. S waves shake rock side to side and can only travel through solids. When seismographs around the world record a large earthquake, there is a broad zone on the far side of the planet where S waves never arrive. Something liquid is blocking them: the outer core.

In 1936 the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann noticed faint P waves arriving in a zone where none were expected. She proposed that they were bouncing off a solid inner core, and she was right.

Field note

Kola Superdeep Borehole, Russia

Soviet scientists started drilling here in 1970. By 1989 they had reached 12,262 meters, where the rock was about 180 °C, far hotter than expected. The heat made the rock behave almost like plastic, and the drill could go no further. It is still the deepest hole humans have ever made, and it barely got a third of the way through the continental crust.

Key terms

Differentiation
The sorting of a planet into layers by density, with the heaviest material sinking to the center.
Lithosphere
The rigid outer shell: the crust plus the uppermost mantle. It is broken into tectonic plates.
Asthenosphere
The weak, slowly flowing layer of upper mantle that the plates ride on.
Seismic wave
Energy that travels through Earth after an earthquake. Its speed and path reveal what it passes through.
P wave
A compression wave. The fastest seismic wave; travels through solids and liquids.
S wave
A shear wave. Slower than P waves, and cannot travel through liquids.

Q1Why is the inner core solid even though it is hotter than the outer core?
Pressure. The enormous pressure at the center raises the melting point of iron above the actual temperature, so the inner core stays solid. The outer core is under less pressure, so it is liquid.
Q2A seismograph on the far side of Earth records P waves from an earthquake but no S waves. What does that tell you?
S waves can't travel through liquid. Their absence on the far side shows that a liquid layer, the outer core, lies in their path.
Q3Continental crust is thicker than oceanic crust. Which one is denser, and why does that matter?
Oceanic crust (basalt) is denser. When the two collide, the denser oceanic crust sinks beneath the continent. You'll see why that matters in Week 3.

Three ways to make a rock

Igneous rocks crystallize from molten rock. Cooling speed controls crystal size. Magma that cools slowly deep underground grows large crystals you can see, as in granite. Lava that cools quickly at the surface makes tiny crystals, as in basalt, or even glass, as in obsidian.

Sedimentary rocks form at the surface. Older rock is broken down by weathering, and the pieces are carried by water, wind or ice and laid down in layers. Over time the layers are buried, compacted and cemented into sandstone, shale or limestone. Because they form at ordinary temperatures, sedimentary rocks are where almost all fossils are found.

Metamorphic rocks are existing rocks transformed by heat and pressure without melting. Minerals recrystallize and often line up in bands called foliation. Shale becomes slate, then schist, then gneiss as conditions intensify. Limestone becomes marble.

A cycle with no beginning

In the 1780s the Scottish farmer and naturalist James Hutton realized that these processes connect. Mountains erode into sediment, sediment becomes rock, rock is buried and transformed or melted, and new rock is pushed back up to erode again.

Hutton concluded that Earth must be unimaginably old for this cycle to have run so many times. He wrote that he found "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." That idea, now called deep time, is the foundation of this course.

Field note

Siccar Point, Scotland

In 1788 Hutton took two friends by boat to this headland. There, nearly vertical layers of gray rock are capped by gently sloping red sandstone. The lower layers had to be deposited, buried, tilted on end, lifted up and worn flat before the upper layers were laid on top. Hutton's companion John Playfair later wrote that "the mind seemed to grow giddy by looking so far into the abyss of time."

Key terms

Intrusive
Igneous rock that cooled underground. Slow cooling gives large crystals.
Extrusive
Igneous rock that cooled at the surface. Fast cooling gives small crystals or glass.
Lithification
The process of compacting and cementing loose sediment into solid rock.
Clastic
Sedimentary rock built from fragments of other rocks, like sandstone.
Foliation
Parallel bands or sheets in a metamorphic rock, caused by minerals aligning under pressure.
Parent rock
The original rock that was transformed into a metamorphic rock.

Q1You find two igneous rocks. One has crystals you can see, and the other has crystals too small to see. Which one cooled underground?
The one with visible crystals. Slow cooling underground gives crystals time to grow large.
Q2Why are fossils almost never found in igneous rock?
Molten rock destroys organic remains. Fossils are preserved when organisms are buried in sediment at normal surface temperatures.
Q3Marble and limestone are both made mostly of the mineral calcite. What's the difference?
Marble is limestone that has been recrystallized by heat and pressure. Its crystals interlock, and the original grains, pores and fossils are erased.

From drift to plates

In 1912 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined in a single landmass and had since drifted apart. His evidence was strong: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together, the same fossil reptile, Mesosaurus, appears on both, and ancient glacial deposits turn up in places that are now tropical.

Most geologists rejected the idea because Wegener couldn't explain what moved the continents. The answer came from the seafloor. In the 1950s Marie Tharp, working with Bruce Heezen, mapped a deep rift valley running down the center of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Magnetic surveys then showed matching stripes of rock on either side of the ridges, recording flips in Earth's magnetic field as new crust formed and spread outward.

The seafloor is young. Almost none of it is older than about 200 million years, because it is constantly made at ridges and destroyed elsewhere. By the late 1960s these discoveries had come together as plate tectonics: Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into plates that move a few centimeters a year.

Where plates meet

Divergent boundaries are where plates pull apart. Magma rises to fill the gap and forms new crust, as along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and in Iceland.

Convergent boundaries are where plates collide. When oceanic crust meets a continent, the denser ocean plate sinks back into the mantle, a process called subduction. That creates deep ocean trenches and chains of volcanoes like the Andes. When two continents collide, neither sinks easily, and the crust crumples into huge mountain ranges like the Himalaya.

Transform boundaries are where plates slide past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed, but the plates lock and slip, causing frequent earthquakes. California's San Andreas Fault is the best-known example.

Field note

Þingvellir, Iceland

In Þingvellir National Park you can walk along a rift valley where the North American and Eurasian plates are pulling apart by about 2 cm a year. In the Silfra fissure, divers swim through clear glacial meltwater in the crack between the two plates.

Key terms

Plate
A rigid slab of lithosphere that moves as a unit over the asthenosphere.
Subduction
The sinking of one plate beneath another into the mantle at a convergent boundary.
Mid-ocean ridge
An undersea mountain chain where plates diverge and new oceanic crust forms.
Trench
A deep, narrow valley in the ocean floor where a plate is subducting.
Seafloor spreading
The creation of new oceanic crust at ridges and its movement away from them.
Paleomagnetism
The record of Earth's past magnetic field preserved in rocks.

Q1The oldest continental rocks are about 4 billion years old, but the oldest seafloor is only about 200 million years old. Why the difference?
Oceanic crust is dense and is recycled into the mantle at subduction zones. Continental crust is too light to sink, so very old pieces of it survive.
Q2The Andes have many active volcanoes, but the Himalaya have almost none. Why?
The Andes sit above an oceanic plate subducting under a continent, which melts mantle rock and feeds volcanoes. The Himalaya formed where two continents collided, and no plate is subducting deep enough to make magma.
Q3Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Is it getting wider or narrower?
Wider, by about 2 cm a year, because the plates on either side are moving apart.

Stored strain, sudden release

Plates don't glide smoothly. Along a fault, friction locks the rocks together while the plates keep moving, so the rock slowly bends and stores energy. When the stress finally overcomes friction, the fault slips and the rock snaps back. This idea, called elastic rebound, came from studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

The point underground where the slip begins is the focus. The point on the surface directly above it is the epicenter.

Scientists measure earthquake size with the moment magnitude scale. It is logarithmic: each step up of one whole number releases about 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7 releases roughly 1,000 times the energy of a magnitude 5.

Why volcanoes behave differently

Whether a volcano erupts gently or violently depends mostly on how thick, or viscous, its magma is. Viscosity depends on silica content and dissolved gas.

Low-silica basalt magma is runny. Gas bubbles escape easily, so the lava pours out in flows and fountains, as at Kīlauea in Hawai‘i.

High-silica magma is sticky and traps gas. Pressure builds until the magma shatters in an explosion that throws out ash and sends avalanches of hot gas and rock, called pyroclastic flows, racing downhill. Mount St. Helens in 1980 and Mount Pinatubo in 1991 were eruptions of this kind.

Field note

Mount St. Helens, Washington

On May 18, 1980, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake shook loose the bulging north face of the volcano, causing the largest landslide in recorded history. With the weight suddenly removed, the gas-rich magma inside blasted sideways and flattened about 600 km² of forest. The mountain lost about 400 meters of height in a single morning.

Key terms

Fault
A fracture in rock along which the two sides have moved.
Focus
The point underground where an earthquake rupture begins. Also called the hypocenter.
Epicenter
The point on Earth's surface directly above the focus.
Elastic rebound
The sudden snapping back of bent rock when a locked fault slips.
Viscosity
A fluid's resistance to flow. High-viscosity magma is thick and sticky.
Pyroclastic flow
A fast-moving avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock from an explosive eruption.

Putting events in order

In 1669 the Danish scientist Nicolas Steno set out simple rules that geologists still use. Superposition: in undisturbed layers, the oldest is at the bottom and the youngest at the top. Original horizontality: sediment settles in flat layers, so tilted or folded layers were disturbed later.

Cross-cutting relationships add another rule: anything that cuts through rock layers, such as a fault or a vein of igneous rock, must be younger than the layers it cuts.

Sometimes layers are missing. An unconformity is a surface where rock was eroded away, or never deposited, before the next layers formed on top. Each one is a gap in the record.

Putting numbers on time

Relative dating gives order but not ages. Real ages come from radioactive isotopes, which decay into other elements at a steady, known rate. The half-life is the time it takes for half of a sample's parent isotope to decay.

By measuring how much parent isotope remains compared with the daughter product it decays into, scientists can calculate how long the clock has been running.

Different isotopes suit different jobs. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years and dates once-living material up to about 50,000 years old. Uranium-238 has a half-life of 4.5 billion years and is used to date the oldest rocks on Earth, including mineral grains 4.4 billion years old.

Field note

Grand Canyon, Arizona

Nearly two billion years of history are exposed in the canyon walls. At the Great Unconformity, flat layers of sandstone around 500 million years old sit directly on top of schist that is about 1.75 billion years old. In places, more than a billion years of rock record is simply gone.

Key terms

Superposition
In undisturbed layers, older rocks lie below younger rocks.
Original horizontality
Sediment is deposited in flat layers. Tilted layers were moved after they formed.
Cross-cutting relationships
A feature that cuts through rock is younger than the rock it cuts.
Unconformity
A buried surface of erosion or non-deposition: a gap in the rock record.
Isotope
A form of an element with a particular number of neutrons. Some isotopes are radioactive.
Half-life
The time for half of a radioactive parent isotope to decay.

Q1A vertical band of basalt cuts through three sandstone layers. Which is older, the basalt or the sandstone?
All three sandstone layers are older. The basalt cut through them, so it must have come later.
Q2A sample has 25% of its original parent isotope left. How many half-lives have passed?
Two. After one half-life 50% remains, and after two, 25%.
Q3Why can't carbon-14 be used to date a dinosaur bone?
Dinosaurs lived more than 66 million years ago. After about 50,000 years too little carbon-14 is left to measure. Geologists instead date volcanic ash layers above and below the fossil using longer-lived isotopes.

How to become a fossil

Most living things leave no trace. To become a fossil, an organism usually needs hard parts such as shell, bone or wood, and quick burial in sediment that keeps out oxygen and scavengers.

Fossils come in several kinds. Body fossils preserve the organism itself, often with its original material replaced by minerals. Trace fossils such as footprints, burrows and bite marks record behavior. Molds and casts preserve an organism's shape after the original has dissolved.

Some fossils are especially useful for dating. An index fossil comes from a species that was widespread and common but lived for only a short time. Finding the same index fossil in two places means the rocks are about the same age. In 1815 William Smith used fossils this way to make the first geologic map of an entire country, England and Wales.

Five times the world nearly ended

Species go extinct all the time. A mass extinction is different: a large share of all species disappearing in a geologically short time. The rock record shows five of them, known as the Big Five.

The largest was the end-Permian extinction 252 million years ago, sometimes called the Great Dying. Massive volcanic eruptions in Siberia released enormous amounts of carbon dioxide, warming and acidifying the oceans. Roughly nine in ten marine species vanished.

The most famous is the end-Cretaceous extinction 66 million years ago, when an asteroid about 10 km across struck what is now Mexico and ended the age of non-bird dinosaurs. After each extinction, survivors spread into the empty niches. Mammals, small and few before, diversified rapidly after the dinosaurs were gone.

Field note

Gubbio, Italy

In the 1970s geologist Walter Alvarez studied a thin layer of clay in the limestone near Gubbio that marks the end of the Cretaceous. With his father Luis, a physicist, he found that it held far more iridium than normal. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. Their 1980 paper proposed an impact, and in 1991 the crater was identified: Chicxulub, buried beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula.

Key terms

Body fossil
The preserved remains of an organism, such as a shell, bone or leaf.
Trace fossil
Evidence of an organism's activity, such as footprints, burrows or droppings.
Index fossil
A fossil of a widespread, short-lived species, used to match rock layers by age.
Mass extinction
The loss of a large share of Earth's species in a geologically short time.
Adaptive radiation
The rapid diversification of a group into many new species, often after an extinction.
K–Pg boundary
The rock layer marking the end of the Cretaceous Period, 66 million years ago.

Reading old climates

Thermometers only go back a few centuries, so scientists use proxies: natural records that respond to climate. Tree rings, pollen in lake mud, the chemistry of seashells and the shapes of glacial deposits all preserve clues about past temperatures and rainfall.

Ice cores are especially powerful. As snow piles up on ice sheets and compresses into ice, it traps tiny bubbles of air. Each bubble is a sample of the atmosphere from the time it was sealed. The chemistry of the ice itself also records the temperature when the snow fell.

What changes the climate

Over tens of thousands of years, small, regular changes in Earth's orbit and tilt, called Milankovitch cycles, change how sunlight is spread across the planet. They repeat roughly every 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years, and they set the rhythm of the ice ages.

Greenhouse gases amplify those changes. When the orbit warms the planet slightly, the oceans release carbon dioxide, which traps more heat. Over millions of years, moving continents and volcanic activity matter too. Around 700 million years ago, Earth may have frozen over almost entirely, an episode called Snowball Earth.

Today, carbon dioxide is above 420 parts per million. That is higher than at any point in the 800,000-year ice-core record, and it is rising much faster than the natural changes recorded there.

Field note

Dome C, Antarctica

Scientists from the European EPICA project drilled 3,270 meters down into the ice at Dome C. The deepest ice fell as snow about 800,000 years ago. Air bubbles from the core record eight ice-age cycles, with carbon dioxide rising and falling between roughly 180 and 300 ppm.

Key terms

Proxy
A natural record, such as tree rings or ice, used to estimate past climate.
Ice core
A long cylinder of ice drilled from a glacier or ice sheet, layered by year.
Milankovitch cycles
Regular changes in Earth's orbit and tilt that pace the ice ages.
Greenhouse effect
The warming of Earth's surface by gases that trap outgoing heat.
Interglacial
A warmer period between ice ages. We are living in one now.
Snowball Earth
Episodes about 700 million years ago when ice may have covered nearly the whole planet.

A human layer

Geologists divide Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and epochs, each defined by changes in the rock record. We officially live in the Holocene epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago at the end of the last ice age.

Many scientists argue that human activity has changed the planet enough to define a new unit, the Anthropocene. Future geologists would find plenty of evidence: plutonium from nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s, particles of plastic and concrete, ash from burning coal, and the jump in carbon dioxide trapped in ice.

To define a new unit, geologists choose a single reference point in the rock called a golden spike. In 2024 the committee responsible voted against making the Anthropocene an official epoch, partly because human effects began at different times in different places. The term is still widely used, and the debate continues.

All of time on one calendar

Earth's 4.54-billion-year history is hard to picture. One way is to squeeze it into a single calendar year, starting with Earth's formation at midnight on January 1. Each day then stands for about 12.4 million years.

On that calendar, life appears in early March, but complex animals don't show up until mid-November. Dinosaurs disappear the day after Christmas. Our species arrives late on the evening of December 31, and all of recorded history fits into the last minute.

Field note

Crawford Lake, Ontario

This small, deep lake has bottom water that almost never mixes, so each year's sediment settles in its own thin layer, like tree rings. Cores from the lake show plutonium appearing sharply in the early 1950s. In 2023 scientists proposed the lake as the golden spike for the Anthropocene. The proposal was voted down in 2024, but the lake's layers remain one of the clearest records of human impact anywhere.

Key terms

Anthropocene
A proposed name for the current interval of time, defined by human impact on Earth.
Holocene
The current official epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago.
Golden spike
The single reference point in rock that marks the start of a geologic time unit. Officially a GSSP.
Epoch
A subdivision of a geologic period, such as the Holocene or the Pleistocene.
Technofossil
A human-made object likely to be preserved in the rock record, such as plastic or concrete.
Stratigraphic marker
A distinctive signal in rock layers that can be traced across the globe.
