How to become a fossil

Most living things leave no trace. To become a fossil, an organism usually needs hard parts such as shell, bone or wood, and quick burial in sediment that keeps out oxygen and scavengers.

Fossils come in several kinds. Body fossils preserve the organism itself, often with its original material replaced by minerals. Trace fossils such as footprints, burrows and bite marks record behavior. Molds and casts preserve an organism's shape after the original has dissolved.

Some fossils are especially useful for dating. An index fossil comes from a species that was widespread and common but lived for only a short time. Finding the same index fossil in two places means the rocks are about the same age. In 1815 William Smith used fossils this way to make the first geologic map of an entire country, England and Wales.

Five times the world nearly ended

Species go extinct all the time. A mass extinction is different: a large share of all species disappearing in a geologically short time. The rock record shows five of them, known as the Big Five.

The largest was the end-Permian extinction 252 million years ago, sometimes called the Great Dying. Massive volcanic eruptions in Siberia released enormous amounts of carbon dioxide, warming and acidifying the oceans. Roughly nine in ten marine species vanished.

The most famous is the end-Cretaceous extinction 66 million years ago, when an asteroid about 10 km across struck what is now Mexico and ended the age of non-bird dinosaurs. After each extinction, survivors spread into the empty niches. Mammals, small and few before, diversified rapidly after the dinosaurs were gone.

Field note

Gubbio, Italy

In the 1970s geologist Walter Alvarez studied a thin layer of clay in the limestone near Gubbio that marks the end of the Cretaceous. With his father Luis, a physicist, he found that it held far more iridium than normal. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. Their 1980 paper proposed an impact, and in 1991 the crater was identified: Chicxulub, buried beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula.

Key terms

Body fossil
The preserved remains of an organism, such as a shell, bone or leaf.
Trace fossil
Evidence of an organism's activity, such as footprints, burrows or droppings.
Index fossil
A fossil of a widespread, short-lived species, used to match rock layers by age.
Mass extinction
The loss of a large share of Earth's species in a geologically short time.
Adaptive radiation
The rapid diversification of a group into many new species, often after an extinction.
K–Pg boundary
The rock layer marking the end of the Cretaceous Period, 66 million years ago.

Check your understanding

Try each question before you open the answer. These aren't graded.

Q1Which makes a better index fossil: a species that lived for 50 million years in a single lake, or one that lived for 1 million years in oceans worldwide?
The ocean species. It was widespread, so it can match rocks across continents, and short-lived, so it pins down a narrow window of time.
Q2Why are soft-bodied animals like jellyfish so rare in the fossil record?
With no hard parts, they usually decay or are eaten before they can be buried and preserved.
Q3What evidence pointed to an asteroid impact at the end of the Cretaceous?
A worldwide clay layer enriched in iridium, shocked quartz grains and glassy spherules in that layer, and the Chicxulub crater, which is dated to about 66 million years ago.
