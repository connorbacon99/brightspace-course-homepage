A human layer

Geologists divide Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and epochs, each defined by changes in the rock record. We officially live in the Holocene epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago at the end of the last ice age.

Many scientists argue that human activity has changed the planet enough to define a new unit, the Anthropocene. Future geologists would find plenty of evidence: plutonium from nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s, particles of plastic and concrete, ash from burning coal, and the jump in carbon dioxide trapped in ice.

To define a new unit, geologists choose a single reference point in the rock called a golden spike. In 2024 the committee responsible voted against making the Anthropocene an official epoch, partly because human effects began at different times in different places. The term is still widely used, and the debate continues.

All of time on one calendar

Earth's 4.54-billion-year history is hard to picture. One way is to squeeze it into a single calendar year, starting with Earth's formation at midnight on January 1. Each day then stands for about 12.4 million years.

On that calendar, life appears in early March, but complex animals don't show up until mid-November. Dinosaurs disappear the day after Christmas. Our species arrives late on the evening of December 31, and all of recorded history fits into the last minute.

Field note

Crawford Lake, Ontario

This small, deep lake has bottom water that almost never mixes, so each year's sediment settles in its own thin layer, like tree rings. Cores from the lake show plutonium appearing sharply in the early 1950s. In 2023 scientists proposed the lake as the golden spike for the Anthropocene. The proposal was voted down in 2024, but the lake's layers remain one of the clearest records of human impact anywhere.

Key terms

Anthropocene
A proposed name for the current interval of time, defined by human impact on Earth.
Holocene
The current official epoch, which began about 11,700 years ago.
Golden spike
The single reference point in rock that marks the start of a geologic time unit. Officially a GSSP.
Epoch
A subdivision of a geologic period, such as the Holocene or the Pleistocene.
Technofossil
A human-made object likely to be preserved in the rock record, such as plastic or concrete.
Stratigraphic marker
A distinctive signal in rock layers that can be traced across the globe.

Check your understanding

Try each question before you open the answer. These aren't graded.

Q1Why is plutonium from 1950s weapons tests such a useful marker?
It spread around the whole globe, it appears suddenly in a known year, and almost none existed naturally before then.
Q2If human impact is so clear, why wasn't the Anthropocene made an official epoch?
The committee had concerns about defining it by a single date in the 1950s, when human effects on Earth built up gradually over thousands of years and began at different times in different places.
Q3On the one-year calendar, what date do the non-bird dinosaurs disappear?
December 26, about 66 million years ago.
