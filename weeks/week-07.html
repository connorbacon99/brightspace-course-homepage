Reading old climates

Thermometers only go back a few centuries, so scientists use proxies: natural records that respond to climate. Tree rings, pollen in lake mud, the chemistry of seashells and the shapes of glacial deposits all preserve clues about past temperatures and rainfall.

Ice cores are especially powerful. As snow piles up on ice sheets and compresses into ice, it traps tiny bubbles of air. Each bubble is a sample of the atmosphere from the time it was sealed. The chemistry of the ice itself also records the temperature when the snow fell.

What changes the climate

Over tens of thousands of years, small, regular changes in Earth's orbit and tilt, called Milankovitch cycles, change how sunlight is spread across the planet. They repeat roughly every 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years, and they set the rhythm of the ice ages.

Greenhouse gases amplify those changes. When the orbit warms the planet slightly, the oceans release carbon dioxide, which traps more heat. Over millions of years, moving continents and volcanic activity matter too. Around 700 million years ago, Earth may have frozen over almost entirely, an episode called Snowball Earth.

Today, carbon dioxide is above 420 parts per million. That is higher than at any point in the 800,000-year ice-core record, and it is rising much faster than the natural changes recorded there.

Field note

Dome C, Antarctica

Scientists from the European EPICA project drilled 3,270 meters down into the ice at Dome C. The deepest ice fell as snow about 800,000 years ago. Air bubbles from the core record eight ice-age cycles, with carbon dioxide rising and falling between roughly 180 and 300 ppm.

Key terms

Proxy
A natural record, such as tree rings or ice, used to estimate past climate.
Ice core
A long cylinder of ice drilled from a glacier or ice sheet, layered by year.
Milankovitch cycles
Regular changes in Earth's orbit and tilt that pace the ice ages.
Greenhouse effect
The warming of Earth's surface by gases that trap outgoing heat.
Interglacial
A warmer period between ice ages. We are living in one now.
Snowball Earth
Episodes about 700 million years ago when ice may have covered nearly the whole planet.

Check your understanding

Try each question before you open the answer. These aren't graded.

Q1How can a bubble in ancient ice tell us about the past atmosphere?
When snow compresses into ice, it seals small pockets of air. Measuring the gases in those bubbles gives a direct sample of the atmosphere from that time.
Q2Name the three Milankovitch cycles' approximate lengths.
About 100,000 years, 41,000 years and 23,000 years.
Q3How does today's carbon dioxide level compare with the 800,000-year ice-core record?
It is above 420 ppm. Before the industrial era, the ice-core record never went higher than about 300 ppm.
