A planet in layers

Earth is not the same all the way through. When the young planet was hot enough to be partly molten, heavy iron sank toward the center and lighter rock rose toward the surface. Geologists call this sorting differentiation, and it left Earth in three main layers.

The crust is the thin outer skin. Continental crust is made mostly of granite-like rock and averages about 35 km thick, reaching 70 km under big mountain ranges. Oceanic crust is denser basalt, only 5 to 10 km thick.

The mantle is about 2,900 km thick and makes up roughly 84% of Earth's volume. It is solid rock, but over millions of years it flows very slowly, like extremely stiff putty. That slow flow drives the plate motions we'll study in Unit 2.

The core is mostly iron and nickel. The outer core is liquid, and its swirling motion generates Earth's magnetic field. The inner core is solid even though it is about 5,200 °C, close to the temperature of the Sun's surface. The pressure there is so great that the metal can't melt.

Seeing through solid rock

Nobody has ever been to the mantle. The deepest hole ever drilled reaches about 12 km, which is 0.2% of the way to the center. Almost everything we know about the deep interior comes from earthquakes.

Earthquakes send out two main kinds of body waves. P waves are compression waves that can travel through solids and liquids. S waves shake rock side to side and can only travel through solids. When seismographs around the world record a large earthquake, there is a broad zone on the far side of the planet where S waves never arrive. Something liquid is blocking them: the outer core.

In 1936 the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann noticed faint P waves arriving in a zone where none were expected. She proposed that they were bouncing off a solid inner core, and she was right.

Field note

Kola Superdeep Borehole, Russia

Soviet scientists started drilling here in 1970. By 1989 they had reached 12,262 meters, where the rock was about 180 °C, far hotter than expected. The heat made the rock behave almost like plastic, and the drill could go no further. It is still the deepest hole humans have ever made, and it barely got a third of the way through the continental crust.

Key terms

Differentiation
The sorting of a planet into layers by density, with the heaviest material sinking to the center.
Lithosphere
The rigid outer shell: the crust plus the uppermost mantle. It is broken into tectonic plates.
Asthenosphere
The weak, slowly flowing layer of upper mantle that the plates ride on.
Seismic wave
Energy that travels through Earth after an earthquake. Its speed and path reveal what it passes through.
P wave
A compression wave. The fastest seismic wave; travels through solids and liquids.
S wave
A shear wave. Slower than P waves, and cannot travel through liquids.

Check your understanding

Try each question before you open the answer. These aren't graded.

Q1Why is the inner core solid even though it is hotter than the outer core?
Pressure. The enormous pressure at the center raises the melting point of iron above the actual temperature, so the inner core stays solid. The outer core is under less pressure, so it is liquid.
Q2A seismograph on the far side of Earth records P waves from an earthquake but no S waves. What does that tell you?
S waves can't travel through liquid. Their absence on the far side shows that a liquid layer, the outer core, lies in their path.
Q3Continental crust is thicker than oceanic crust. Which one is denser, and why does that matter?
Oceanic crust (basalt) is denser. When the two collide, the denser oceanic crust sinks beneath the continent. You'll see why that matters in Week 3.
