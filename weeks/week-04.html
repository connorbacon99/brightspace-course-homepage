Stored strain, sudden release

Plates don't glide smoothly. Along a fault, friction locks the rocks together while the plates keep moving, so the rock slowly bends and stores energy. When the stress finally overcomes friction, the fault slips and the rock snaps back. This idea, called elastic rebound, came from studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

The point underground where the slip begins is the focus. The point on the surface directly above it is the epicenter.

Scientists measure earthquake size with the moment magnitude scale. It is logarithmic: each step up of one whole number releases about 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7 releases roughly 1,000 times the energy of a magnitude 5.

Why volcanoes behave differently

Whether a volcano erupts gently or violently depends mostly on how thick, or viscous, its magma is. Viscosity depends on silica content and dissolved gas.

Low-silica basalt magma is runny. Gas bubbles escape easily, so the lava pours out in flows and fountains, as at Kīlauea in Hawai‘i.

High-silica magma is sticky and traps gas. Pressure builds until the magma shatters in an explosion that throws out ash and sends avalanches of hot gas and rock, called pyroclastic flows, racing downhill. Mount St. Helens in 1980 and Mount Pinatubo in 1991 were eruptions of this kind.

Field note

Mount St. Helens, Washington

On May 18, 1980, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake shook loose the bulging north face of the volcano, causing the largest landslide in recorded history. With the weight suddenly removed, the gas-rich magma inside blasted sideways and flattened about 600 km² of forest. The mountain lost about 400 meters of height in a single morning.

Key terms

Fault
A fracture in rock along which the two sides have moved.
Focus
The point underground where an earthquake rupture begins. Also called the hypocenter.
Epicenter
The point on Earth's surface directly above the focus.
Elastic rebound
The sudden snapping back of bent rock when a locked fault slips.
Viscosity
A fluid's resistance to flow. High-viscosity magma is thick and sticky.
Pyroclastic flow
A fast-moving avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock from an explosive eruption.

Check your understanding

Try each question before you open the answer. These aren't graded.

Q1About how much more energy does a magnitude 8 earthquake release than a magnitude 6?
About 1,000 times. Each step is about 32 times more energy, and 32 × 32 ≈ 1,000.
Q2Why are Hawaiian eruptions usually much less explosive than the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens?
Hawaiian magma is low-silica basalt. It is runny, so gas escapes easily instead of building up pressure.
Q3News reports give an earthquake's location as a point on a map. Is that the focus or the epicenter?
The epicenter, the point on the surface above where the rupture began. The focus is underground.
